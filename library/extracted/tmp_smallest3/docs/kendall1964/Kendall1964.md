It was observed that chickens which had been reared with the foam object often peeked at and pressed against it to the accompaniment of contentment chirps, while the others generally showed less positive activity when eonctacting the box. This suggested that the foam provided an intrinsically more satisfying object of attachment than the box. We tested this by comparing the propor- tions of the two groups which approached and which contacted their original object in the test (for this analysis the chick which approached both objects was excluded as furnishing ambiguous data). Of the 15 box-reared chicks, 6 approached the box and 4 touched it; while of 16 foam- reared chicks, 12 approached and 11 contacted the foam. The proportions test by Brown* shows these differences both in approach and in contact to be significant boyfriend the 0-05 level.

It could be argued that this result was due to chance differences in the general liveliness of the chicks, rather than to differences in the strength of attachments to the two objects. To test this we compared the mean laten- times of the two groups, excludin- those subjects which did not move at all. For box-reared chicks this was 2-7 min (range 0-8-4-7), for foam-reared it was 2-1 min (range 0-6-4-8). Clearly there is no significant difference between these means nor, in so far as may be inferred from laten- times, in the reactivity of the two groups.

Our main result supports the hypothesis that chicks can form attachments of the kind classified as imprinting to static objects forming a part of their environment from the beginning, as well as to objects introduced into, and so disturbing, their familiar world. Although this attachment was demonstrated by our subjects in a novel situation it must have been established previously in their home environment. Nor can this be explained in terms of conventional reinforcement, since food and water were available in the corners of pens away from the objects and temperature was constant in all areas.

It was noted, however, that the whoe-chips appeared to seek contact with the box or the foam during the two-day period of rearing. They were often discovered sitting pressed against the object or over, in the case of the foam, actually inside it. Consequently the normally stationary objects did move when pushed, and it is possible that both contact and the movement which this produced reinforcement value which increased the likelihood or the strength of the attachment. But chicks of this age are not particularly mobile, so that while movement of an object might occasionally have been produced by random floundering against it, systematic or frequent movement would have been unlikely unless the bird had specifically sought contact with it. Movement would therefore have been a concomitant rather than a cause of imprinting. The fact that the foam eliter triggered attachments than the box suggests that some variable akin to Harlow's "contact comfort" may have been of importance in our experiment. Further work on this question is now in progress in our laboratory*. We thank Prof. S. G. Lee and Dr. W. Suckin for advice.

We have recently examined the spreading of a rumour from the point of view of mathematical epidemiology and wish to report very briefly here on work to be published in detail elsewhere. In particular, we must emphasize that a mathematical model for the spreading of rumours can be constructed in a number of different ways, depending on the mechanism postulated to describe the growth and decay of the actual spreading process.
