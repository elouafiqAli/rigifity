Dear Annalisa Crannell,

I am submitting my article \textit{The Continuous-Coefficient Jensen
Equation: How Real Mixing Weights Retire Regularity} for consideration
by \textit{The American Mathematical Monthly}. The work is original,
unpublished, and not under consideration elsewhere.

At the center of the paper is a small but decisive reversal. A century
of analysis has taught readers to expect regularity hypotheses---
continuity, measurability, monotonicity, boundedness---before Jensen's
equation yields an affine conclusion. That instinct is exactly right
for rational weights and exactly wrong once the mixing weight ranges
over the full interval $[0,1]$. In that setting, a single endpoint
substitution forces the solution onto its chord, with no regularity
hypothesis at all.

The manuscript treats that one-line observation as the entrance to a
larger landscape. It draws a sharp boundary between settings in which
the classical hypotheses still do real work and settings in which they
have quietly become vestigial; explains why Hamel pathology survives the
rational tests yet fails at irrational weights; and gives the reader a
compact diagnostic for telling those situations apart. The aim is not
just to settle the equation, but to show why one extra degree of mixing
changes the logic so completely.

This seems well suited to \textit{Monthly}. The proof is brief and
transparent, the central question is easy to state, and the payoff is
wider than the calculation itself: a familiar functional equation turns
out to separate several neighboring literatures in an unexpectedly
clean way. The article is written for readers who enjoy a result that
can be grasped quickly and then keeps unfolding.
